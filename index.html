A simple guide to makeup for the younger me.

System of Beauty is a design study that examines the makeup routine as a framework for understanding beauty culture and its design histories. Presented through a physical accordion publication and a corresponding digital timeline, the project uses the familiar sequence of cosmetic application to organize research on the cultural and societal values embedded in each product. By tracing how everyday tools of self-presentation have evolved, both materially and ideologically, the work reframes a private ritual as a collective reflection on how design perpetuates ideals of beauty, identity, and transformation.

Foundation

CONTEXT

→

Foundation in relation to the Asian American experience reflects a larger story about racialized beauty standards and the struggle for visibility within Western culture. For most of the 20th century, American cosmetics were created with white consumers in mind, shaped by Eurocentric ideals that celebrated fair skin and narrow, angular features.

Scholars such as Thuy Linh Nguyen Tu (The Beautiful Generation, 2011) and Minh Ha T Pham (Asians Wear Clothes on the Internet, 2015) note that this beauty culture marginalized Asian faces and complexions, offering few shades or techniques suited to the undertones and features common among Asian communities.

Before the early 2000s, foundation lines rarely included colors that matched Asian skin. Many brands offered only pink based ivory shades or a limited set of deeper tones, without considering yellow, olive, or neutral undertones.

As a result, many Asian American youth learned makeup through trial and error, often adapting products that were never designed for them. Tutorials in mainstream media did not address monolids, flatter nose bridges, or wider cheekbones, reinforcing the sense that Asian features existed outside the standard of beauty (Schlund Vials, Asian American cultural criticism).

The arrival of Asian American beauty creators in the 2010s brought meaningful change. Influencers such as Michelle Phan introduced techniques tailored to Asian features and opened space for new forms of representation (Pham, 2015).

At the same time, the rise of K beauty introduced dewy bases, lighter textures, and expanded shade ranges that influenced the American market (Tu, 2011). For many Asian Americans, choosing foundation became an act of self recognition within a culture that once overlooked them.

TIPS

01

Choose the Right Undertone

Many western foundation brands can lean very orange when you are neutral, olive, or cool toned. Test shades along the jawline for the best match.

02

Start Light, Build Gradually

Apply a thin layer first and build coverage where needed to avoid masking natural contours.

03

Consider Lightweight Formulas

Western foundation formulas can be high coverage and sometimes too cakey. Consider aiming for light coverage, especially for an everyday student routine. BB creams, tinted moisturizers, or lightweight foundations provide coverage without feeling heavy on softer features.

Beauty Practices Among Asian American College Students

The beauty industry is one of the most profitable industries in the United States. At over $382 billion per year, it is also an important component of the global economy. Although many of the successful beauty brands are either from Paris or New York, global sales of beauty products are prominent in East Asian countries like China and Japan. However, the global beauty industry has been criticized for its role in reproducing inequalities, including those related to race, class, and gender. Wolf argues beauty advertisements lower women’s self-confidence about their faces and bodies by using "beautiful" models or actresses. According to Frith, Cheng and Shaw, more white models are used in both U.S. and Asian fashion or beauty magazines. This underrepresentation of Asian models in media does not only make Asian women think that they are "social outsider[s]," but also makes them compare themselves unfavorably to the white group. This indicates racial inequality between whites and Asians and homogenization of beauty standards as western beauty industries expanded their business to East Asia).

The homogenization of western beauty standards recalls Bourdieu's "habitus," a production of a system or a norm which determines practices or actions of certain groups. It is "the product of the work of inculcation" to perpetuate similar characteristics or conventions within individuals' in-groups. In other words, individuals' habitual practices decide their values of society, and the individuals take that for granted. Habitus, "systems of classification, … make their specific contribution to the reproduction of the power relations" and exist through relation and interaction with others. Since the dominant group, who has a certain privilege, decides what is valuable in society, inequality between the dominant group and subordinated groups are created. In the case of beauty standards, western beauty standards interact with non-western countries when they expand the beauty industry worldwide. By encouraging using western models in beauty advertisements, western beauty standards, as a qualifying social value, perpetuate the notion of "ugliness" among people of color and reproduce inequality that people of color should be "beautiful" to be accepted in society. Although there are some Asians who are considered "beautiful" in the media, most have lighter skin, bigger eyes, and ostensibly refined noses—the "right kind of Asian," in that these features are reminiscent of white physical features. The reason why western beauty standards are perpetuated is because of the globalization of the beauty industry. Ashcroft suggests that the terms globalization and western hegemony are synonymous because the diffusion of globalism pressures local identities of non-western nations.

The same holds for globalization of beauty standards because minority groups are usually exposed to western facial and physical features through media, which might influence their favor of beautification practices. East Asian countries are one of the most important beauty markets among non-western countries, because Japan, Korea, and China are ranked among the highest retail sales of beauty products. Despite the fact that the beauty industry stimulates the world economy, as western beauty companies succeed in East Asian countries, it also contributes to suppressing local identities of East Asian cosmetic industries by imposing western standards of beauty through media advertisements and promotion of mega brands. Though some East Asian cosmetic companies offer diversity in beauty ideals by selling cosmetic products which fit Asians, like skin lightener, western beauty standards in many East Asian countries encourage using western models on fashion advertisements or favoring cosmetic products that conceal mono-lid eyes, eyes without folds. As Jones suggests, beauty standards of East Asian countries are quite complex. East Asian countries support western beauty standards because of the expansion of the western beauty industry on one hand, but they perpetuate their local identities on the other. Japan exemplifies this complexity.

Battling Western beauty standards as an Asian American

Lea Yeo

After European colonization of Africa and North and South America began in the 15th century, everything of white, European origins held supreme — from light eyes to blonde hair to pale skin, straight hair, and long, thin noses. As a result, Eurocentric features are what Western nations, including the United States, have considered "conventionally beautiful" for over 500 years.

Western beauty standards dominate mainstream culture. White people are the pinnacle of beauty. Features that primarily exist in people of color, such as larger bodies, broad noses, and small eyes, are often deemed unattractive. As we know it, beauty is a white supremacist ideal that favors people of European descent — an expectation that most individuals have unconsciously accepted.

As a first-generation Korean-American teenager, I struggle to accept my non-Eurocentric features. When all the people I see in movies, magazines, and on billboards are slender and white, I often find myself in a hole of self-pity and having expectations beyond what is truly achievable.

As a child, I desperately wanted my eyes to be a lighter color other than brown. I used to browse the web to find subliminal messages that could supposedly lighten my eye color.

I also recall searching for remedies to make my nose appear slimmer — bookmarking all the YouTube videos I could find that demonstrated specific exercises that could lift the tip of my nose.

After these attempts failed, I searched for rhinoplasty prices in my area. I frequently wondered if the money and months of pain would be worth having the perfectly sloped nose one saw on supermodels. The thought of cosmetic procedures continuously ran through my head as a preteen. In South Korea, where my family is from, plastic surgery is a common topic of conversation for people of all ages and genders.

One example of this is double-eyelid surgery, an invasive cosmetic procedure that makes eyes appear bigger, brighter, and whiter. My mother gave into cultural pressure and underwent this surgery to gain self-confidence. At only 13 years old, my younger sister is contemplating the same surgery.

In countries like Mali, Nigeria, Malaysia, and the Philippines, where citizens often have darker complexions, many people buy skin-whitening cream or carry umbrellas to preserve skin lightness. Due to the hot, sunny weather in these regions, individuals must go to such measures to maintain pale skin, which is considered more respectable and reflects a higher socioeconomic status.

As my white friends boast their Summer tans, I find myself avoiding the sun with a hat. Similarly, many of my Asian friends and family members buy makeup much lighter than their skin tone and dye their hair blonde regularly to appear white.

I've learned that race and ethnicity shouldn't be allowed to negatively dictate how we view our physical appearance. Beauty should be a celebration. Each ethnicity has its own special, unique features. Embracing these characteristics is critical.

There is beauty in diversity.
