A GUIDE TO MAKEUP FOR THE YOUNGER ME

Second Gen Makeup focuses on the kind of makeup representation I needed growing up. As a second generation Korean-American, learning makeup meant years of trial and error because most tutorials were created for Eurocentric features that did not look like mine. Hooded lids, flatter nose bridges, rounder facial structures, and other East Asian were rarely acknowledged, which left people like me to figure things out on our own. In this website and matching publication, I walk through a hypothetical makeup routine step by step, explaining the context of each product and how its use has been shaped through an Asian American lens. I explore the cultural and historical layers behind common makeup techniques, showing how certain methods have evolved differently for East Asian features and why those differences matter. For each product, I also share tips and tricks that may be especially helpful for people who look like me. These are small insights I learned through experience that would have made a difference to my younger self. By centering these perspectives, I hope to offer the representation and guidance I once searched for and to celebrate the beauty that mainstream Western makeup spaces often overlooked.

Foundation

01

Choose the Right Undertone

Many western foundation brands can lean very orange when you are neutral, olive, or cool toned. Test shades along the jawline and neck for the best match. If you have pink or red undertones, seek out "cool" or "rosy" shades.

02

Start Light, Build Gradually

Apply a thin layer first and build coverage where needed to avoid masking natural contours. Generally speaking, less is more when it comes to foundation on softer features. This can also prevent foundation from settling into fine lines.

03

Consider Lightweight Formulas

For an everyday student routine: BB creams, tinted moisturizers, or lightweight foundations provide coverage without feeling heavy on softer features. Remember that your method of application is also important.

Context

Foundation in relation to the Asian American experience reflects a larger story about racialized beauty standards and the struggle for visibility within Western culture. For most of the 20th century, American cosmetics were created with white consumers in mind, shaped by Eurocentric ideals that celebrated fair skin and narrow, angular features. Scholars such as Thuy Linh Nguyen Tu (The Beautiful Generation, 2011) and Minh Ha T Pham (Asians Wear Clothes on the Internet, 2015) note that this beauty culture marginalized Asian faces and complexions, offering few shades or techniques suited to the undertones and features common among Asian communities.

Before the early 2000s, foundation lines rarely included colors that matched Asian skin. Many brands offered only pink based ivory shades or a limited set of deeper tones, without considering yellow, olive, or neutral undertones. As a result, many Asian American youth learned makeup through trial and error, often adapting products that were never designed for them. Tutorials in mainstream media did not address monolids, flatter nose bridges, or wider cheekbones, reinforcing the sense that Asian features existed outside the standard of beauty (Schlund Vials, Asian American cultural criticism).

The arrival of Asian American beauty creators in the 2010s brought meaningful change. Influencers such as Michelle Phan introduced techniques tailored to Asian features and opened space for new forms of representation (Pham, 2015). At the same time, the rise of K beauty introduced dewy bases, lighter textures, and expanded shade ranges that influenced the American market (Tu, 2011). For many Asian Americans, choosing foundation became an act of self recognition within a culture that once overlooked them.
